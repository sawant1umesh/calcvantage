# How Much House Can I Afford on My Salary?

One of the most consequential financial questions you will ever ask yourself is simple: **how much house can I afford on my salary?**

When prospective homebuyers begin exploring the real estate market, they often encounter conflicting answers. An online listing widget might suggest that an $80,000 annual salary can easily afford a $450,000 home. A mortgage lender's pre-approval letter might grant an even larger borrowing limit. Yet when you sit down with your actual monthly bank statements, the reality of property taxes, homeowners insurance, auto loans, and everyday living costs paints a very different picture.

Determining how much house you can realistically afford requires moving beyond crude rules of thumb. Your true purchasing power depends on an interconnected set of financial factors: your gross annual salary, recurring monthly debt obligations, available down payment, prevailing interest rates, and mandatory ancillary housing costs like property taxes and homeowners insurance.

This guide provides a comprehensive, mathematically grounded framework to determine your realistic homebuying budget. We examine traditional mortgage underwriting rules, illustrate affordability benchmarks across salary tiers from $50,000 to $200,000, explain how hidden housing costs reshape your budget, and outline key differences for buyers in the United States, Canada, and Australia.

---

## How Much House Can You Afford on Your Salary?

At the most basic level, your salary establishes the outer boundary of your borrowing capacity. Lenders do not evaluate how much you can afford based on the total purchase price of the home; instead, they evaluate your **monthly cash flow**—specifically, what portion of your monthly income is committed to housing debt.

A widely cited baseline in residential real estate is that a home typically costs between **3.0 to 4.5 times your gross annual salary**, assuming a standard 10% to 20% down payment and moderate interest rates:

* **$50,000 salary:** ~$155,000 to $185,000 purchase budget
* **$75,000 salary:** ~$235,000 to $275,000 purchase budget
* **$100,000 salary:** ~$315,000 to $365,000 purchase budget
* **$150,000 salary:** ~$470,000 to $550,000 purchase budget
* **$200,000 salary:** ~$630,000 to $730,000 purchase budget

However, relying strictly on an income multiplier can be misleading. Two households earning identical $100,000 salaries can have drastically different purchasing power:

1. **Buyer A** has zero student loans, no car payments, a $70,000 down payment, and buys in an area with low property taxes (0.6% annually).
2. **Buyer B** pays $800 per month across student loans and auto financing, has a $25,000 down payment, and buys in a jurisdiction with high property taxes (2.2% annually).

Despite earning the exact same $100,000 salary, Buyer A can comfortably afford a home priced over $380,000, while Buyer B may struggle to afford a $270,000 property without experiencing severe financial strain. 

To bridge the gap between salary and purchase price, mortgage underwriters and financial planners rely on standardized debt service metrics—most notably, the **28/36 rule**.

### 1. The Front-End Ratio (28% Cap)
The front-end ratio mandates that your **total monthly housing payment** should not exceed **28% of your gross monthly income**.

Your gross monthly income is your pre-tax annual salary divided by 12. For instance, if you earn $90,000 per year, your gross monthly income is $7,500. Applying the 28% front-end threshold:

`$7,500 × 0.28 = $2,100 maximum allowable monthly housing payment`

Crucially, this $2,100 limit does not represent just your mortgage principal and interest payment. It must cover your entire **PITI** outlay:
* **P**rincipal (loan balance repayment)
* **I**nterest (borrowing charge)
* **T**axes (local property taxes held in escrow)
* **I**nsurance (homeowners hazard insurance)
* Applicable **PMI** (Private Mortgage Insurance) or **HOA** fees

For a granular breakdown of how each of these line items is structured on your billing statement, review our comprehensive guide on [what is included in a monthly mortgage payment](/blog/understanding-mortgage-payments).

### 2. The Back-End Ratio (36% Cap)
The back-end ratio mandates that your **total monthly debt obligations**—combining your proposed housing expense plus all other recurring debt commitments—should not exceed **36% of your gross monthly income**.

Continuing with the $90,000 annual salary ($7,500 monthly gross):

`$7,500 × 0.36 = $2,700 maximum total debt payment allowance`

This $2,700 ceiling must accommodate:
* Proposed housing payment (PITI + PMI + HOA)
* Monthly auto loan payments
* Minimum required student loan payments
* Minimum monthly credit card payments
* Personal loans, child support, or alimony

If this buyer has $700 in monthly auto and student loan payments, their remaining allowable housing payment under the back-end rule is:

`$2,700 − $700 = $2,000 allowable housing payment`

Because $2,000 (from the back-end calculation) is lower than the $2,100 allowed under the front-end calculation, the **back-end ratio becomes the binding constraint**. The buyer's maximum housing budget is capped at $2,000 per month.

### Is the 28/36 Rule a Hard Lender Requirement?
It is critical to understand that the 28/36 rule is a **conservative personal finance guideline**, not an inflexible federal lending mandate. 

In actual mortgage underwriting:
* **Conventional Conforming Loans:** Guidelines established by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac typically allow total back-end DTIs up to **43% to 45%**, and automated underwriting systems (like Desktop Underwriter) can approve loans with DTIs up to **50%** when strong compensating factors are present (such as substantial cash reserves, high credit scores, or significant equity).
* **FHA Loans:** Insured by the Federal Housing Administration, standard FHA thresholds are **31% front-end / 43% back-end**, but automated underwriting systems routinely approve borrowers with back-end DTIs exceeding **50%**.
* **VA Loans:** Guaranteed by the Department of Veterans Affairs, VA loans do not enforce a rigid front-end cap and benchmark back-end DTI at **41%**, placing primary emphasis on minimum required "residual income" (cash remaining after all monthly obligations to cover food, healthcare, and utilities).

> **Lender Approval vs. Household Comfort:** Just because a commercial mortgage lender is willing to approve your loan at a 45% or 50% back-end debt ratio does not mean it is financially wise to borrow that much. Committing half of your gross pre-tax income to debt service leaves very little margin for savings, emergencies, family expenses, or lifestyle flexibility.

## How Income and Monthly Debt Affect Home Affordability

When calculating how much house you can afford on your salary, your **existing monthly debt obligations** exert a dramatic, dollar-for-dollar drag on your purchasing power.

Because lenders cap total debt service, every dollar committed to existing liabilities directly reduces the money available for your mortgage payment.

### The Mathematics of Debt vs. Borrowing Power
To illustrate the profound impact of non-housing debt, consider how a $100 increase in monthly debt ripples through a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage at a **6.50% interest rate**:

* A monthly principal-and-interest payment of **$6.32** supports approximately **$1,000** in mortgage principal at 6.50%.
* If an auto loan or student loan requires **$500 per month**, that commitment consumes $500 of allowable monthly debt capacity.
* If that $500 were instead allocated to a mortgage payment (covering principal, interest, taxes, and insurance totaling roughly $7.40 per $1,000 of home price), it would support approximately **$67,500 in home purchase price**.

In other words, carrying a $500 monthly car payment can reduce your maximum home purchase budget by nearly **$65,000 to $70,000**!

The table below demonstrates how varying debt levels shift allowable monthly housing payments and resulting home budgets for a household earning **$100,000 annually** ($8,333/month gross), assuming 10% down at 6.50% interest:

| Monthly Non-Housing Debt | Back-End Debt Cap (36%) | Front-End Cap (28%) | Governing Housing Budget | Estimated Max Home Price |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$0 / month** | $3,000 / mo | $2,333 / mo | **$2,333 / mo** (Front-end binding) | **~$315,400** |
| **$300 / month** | $2,700 / mo | $2,333 / mo | **$2,333 / mo** (Front-end binding) | **~$315,400** |
| **$667 / month** | $2,333 / mo | $2,333 / mo | **$2,333 / mo** (Breakeven point) | **~$315,400** |
| **$1,000 / month** | $2,000 / mo | $2,333 / mo | **$2,000 / mo** (Back-end binding) | **~$270,400** |
| **$1,400 / month** | $1,600 / mo | $2,333 / mo | **$1,600 / mo** (Back-end binding) | **~$216,300** |

*Assumptions: 30-year fixed mortgage at 6.50%, 10% down payment, 1.2% annual property taxes, 0.4% annual homeowners insurance, 0.5% annual PMI on loan balance.*

Notice the breakeven inflection point: because the spread between 36% and 28% is exactly **8% of gross salary** ($667/month on a $100,000 salary), monthly debts below $667 do not reduce the buyer's 28% front-end cap under strict guidelines. However, once non-housing debt exceeds $667/month, every additional dollar of debt directly diminishes the maximum home purchase budget.

## How Down Payment and Interest Rates Change Your Budget

While your salary establishes your monthly cash flow ceiling, **down payment size** and **prevailing mortgage interest rates** dictate how much purchase price that cash flow can buy.

### 1. The Impact of Interest Rates
Mortgage interest compounds continuously over decades. When market interest rates rise, borrowing becomes more expensive, and a larger share of each monthly payment is absorbed by interest rather than principal reduction.

Consider a fixed monthly principal-and-interest budget of **$2,000 per month** on a 30-year fixed loan. Observe how purchasing power contracts as mortgage rates shift:

| Mortgage Interest Rate | Maximum Loan Amount Supported | Change vs. 5.50% Benchmark |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **5.50%** | **$352,244** | Baseline |
| **6.00%** | **$333,583** | −$18,661 (−5.3%) |
| **6.50%** | **$316,422** | −$35,822 (−10.2%) |
| **7.00%** | **$300,615** | −$51,629 (−14.7%) |
| **7.50%** | **$286,035** | −$66,209 (−18.8%) |

A 2.00% increase in mortgage rates (from 5.50% to 7.50%) erodes more than **$66,000 in borrowing capacity** on the exact same monthly payment. To maintain your home purchase target when rates climb, you must either increase your down payment, locate a lower-priced home, or increase your annual income. 

To explore the mechanics of how interest accumulates over the life of a loan, consult our educational analysis of [mortgage amortization schedules](/blog/mortgage-amortization-explained) or compare rate options in our overview of [fixed vs variable mortgage rates](/blog/fixed-vs-variable-mortgage-rates).

### 2. The Power of Down Payment
Your down payment influences your home budget across three dimensions:
1. **Loan-to-Value (LTV) Ratio:** A larger down payment reduces the borrowed balance, lowering monthly principal and interest charges.
2. **Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI):** On conventional loans, putting down at least **20%** eliminates the need for PMI, immediately freeing up $80 to $250 per month in payment capacity.
3. **Interest Rate Pricing:** Lenders frequently offer marginally better interest rates to borrowers with lower LTV ratios because the transaction represents lower credit risk.

For example, on a $350,000 home purchase at 6.50% interest:
* **With 5% down ($17,500):** Loan balance is $332,500. Monthly P&I is $2,102, plus ~$138/month in PMI, totaling **$2,240/month** before taxes and insurance.
* **With 20% down ($70,000):** Loan balance is $280,000. Monthly P&I is $1,770, with **$0 PMI**, totaling **$1,770/month** before taxes and insurance.

The 20% down payment saves **$470 per month** in ongoing housing expenses while insulating the homeowner against potential property market declines.

## Property Taxes, Home Insurance, PMI, HOA Fees, and Maintenance

A frequent pitfall for first-time buyers is focusing exclusively on the loan repayment and neglecting ongoing ownership overhead. In many markets, ancillary expenses constitute **30% to 45% of the total monthly housing bill**.

### Property Taxes
Property taxes are assessed by municipal and county governments to fund public education, roads, fire departments, and civic infrastructure. Rates vary enormously by geographic location:
* Low-tax states (e.g., Hawaii, Alabama, Colorado): Property taxes often average **0.3% to 0.6%** of assessed home value per year.
* Average-tax states (e.g., North Carolina, Florida, Arizona): Taxes range between **0.8% and 1.3%** annually.
* High-tax states (e.g., New Jersey, Illinois, Texas, New York): Taxes frequently exceed **1.8% to 2.4%** annually.

On a $400,000 property, an annual tax rate of 0.6% equates to **$200 per month**, whereas a 2.2% tax rate equates to **$733 per month**—a $533 monthly difference on the exact same purchase price.

### Homeowners Insurance
Hazard and casualty insurance protects the physical structure and personal property against fire, storms, and liability. Premiums depend heavily on regional exposure to severe weather (hurricanes, wildfires, tornadoes). Nationally, annual homeowners insurance averages between **0.35% and 0.60%** of home value, or roughly $100 to $200 per month on a $350,000 home, though coastal or high-risk zones can be significantly higher.

### Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI)
If you secure a conventional mortgage with less than a 20% down payment, lenders require **Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI)**. PMI protects the lender if you default on the loan. Annual premiums typically range from **0.30% to 1.15%** of the original loan balance, depending on your credit score and down payment percentage. For a $300,000 loan, expect to pay approximately **$75 to $200 per month** until your loan balance drops to 80% of original value.

### Homeowners Association (HOA) Fees
Condominiums, townhomes, and homes in master-planned communities typically require mandatory monthly or quarterly HOA dues. Dues cover communal exterior maintenance, landscaping, community amenities, and reserve funds. 

> **Important DTI Fact:** Mortgage underwriters factor 100% of your monthly HOA dues into your front-end housing ratio. A $400 monthly HOA fee reduces your borrowing capacity by the exact same amount as borrowing approximately $60,000 in mortgage principal.

### Maintenance and Repair Reserves
While not part of your official mortgage payment, routine home maintenance is an inescapable reality of ownership. Most financial experts recommend setting aside **1% to 2% of the home's total value annually** (or $300 to $600 per month on a $350,000 property) into a dedicated reserve fund for roof replacements, HVAC servicing, plumbing repairs, and exterior painting. 

Before committing your savings to a down payment, ensure you preserve a separate cash reserve. Use our guide to [calculating your emergency fund size](/blog/emergency-fund-size) to ensure your safety net remains intact after closing.

## How to Calculate Your Own Home Budget

You can calculate your personal home affordability target in six systematic steps before contacting a mortgage lender:

### Step 1: Calculate Your Gross Monthly Income
Add up all verifiable pre-tax income sources:
* Base salary or hourly wages (annualized and divided by 12)
* Consistent overtime, commissions, or annual bonuses (typically averaged over a 2-year history by lenders)
* Stable self-employment income (averaged from recent tax returns)

### Step 2: Sum Your Non-Housing Monthly Debts
Review your credit reports and bank statements to total all recurring monthly debt payments:
* Minimum required monthly credit card payments
* Auto loan or vehicle lease payments
* Student loan payments
* Personal loans or lines of credit
* Alimony or child support obligations
*(Do not include variable living expenses like groceries, cell phone bills, or utility costs in this debt total; lenders only count legally binding debt obligations).*

### Step 3: Apply the 28% and 36% Limits
* **Front-end limit:** Multiply your gross monthly income by **0.28**.
* **Back-end limit:** Multiply your gross monthly income by **0.36**, then subtract your total monthly non-housing debt from Step 2.
* **Governing payment limit:** Take the **lower** of the two figures. This is your conservative monthly housing payment ceiling.

### Step 4: Subtract Estimated Escrow and Insurance
From your governing monthly payment limit, deduct estimates for:
* Monthly property taxes (~1.0% to 1.5% of target price ÷ 12)
* Monthly homeowners insurance (~0.4% of target price ÷ 12)
* Monthly PMI (if putting down under 20%, ~0.5% of loan amount ÷ 12)
* Monthly HOA fees (if applicable)

The remainder is your target **monthly principal and interest (P&I)** payment.

### Step 5: Convert P&I to Maximum Loan Amount
Using a standard mortgage amortization formula or our [online mortgage calculator](/mortgage), convert your target monthly P&I payment into a loan balance based on prevailing interest rates.

### Step 6: Add Your Down Payment
Add your available liquid down payment cash (excluding closing costs and your separate emergency fund) to your maximum loan balance to establish your total home purchase price ceiling.

## Lending Rules Differ: Canada and Australia

While the fundamental relationship between income, debt, and mortgage borrowing applies globally, specific regulatory frameworks and underwriting standards differ outside the United States.

### Canada: GDS, TDS, and the B-20 Mortgage Stress Test
In Canada, mortgage lending is regulated primarily by the **Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI)** and the **Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC)**:

1. **Debt Ratios:** Canadian lenders evaluate borrowers using:
   * **Gross Debt Service (GDS):** Maximum **39%** of gross income dedicated to mortgage principal, interest, property taxes, and 50% of condo maintenance fees.
   * **Total Debt Service (TDS):** Maximum **44%** of gross income dedicated to all housing costs plus all other debt obligations.
2. **The Guideline B-20 Stress Test:** Under federal regulations, all borrowers—regardless of down payment size—must qualify at a "qualifying interest rate" equal to either their **contract mortgage rate plus 2.00%**, or **5.25%**—whichever is higher. For example, if your lender offers a 5-year fixed rate of 4.75%, your salary must prove serviceability at **6.75%**. This stress test reduces purchasing power by roughly 15% to 20% compared to unbuffered underwriting.
3. **Down Payment Tiers:** Canada requires minimum down payments on a tiered schedule:
   * 5% on the first $500,000 of purchase price
   * 10% on the portion between $500,000 and $1,500,000
   * 20% on any portion over $1,500,000
   * Any down payment below 20% requires default mortgage insurance from the **Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation (CMHC)**, Sagen, or Canada Guaranty.

### Australia: Serviceability Buffers and APRA Benchmarks
In Australia, home lending standards are governed by the **Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA)** and the **Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC)**:

1. **The 3.00% Serviceability Buffer:** APRA mandates that authorized deposit-taking institutions (banks) assess a borrower's repayment capacity at the loan interest rate plus a **minimum 3.00% interest rate buffer**. If an Australian borrower applies for a home loan at 6.10%, the bank evaluates whether their salary can service repayments at **9.10%**.
2. **Household Expenditure Measure (HEM):** Rather than relying solely on arbitrary debt ratio caps, Australian lenders assess declared living expenses against the **Household Expenditure Measure (HEM)**, a statistical benchmark of modest Australian household spending based on family size and location.
3. **Upfront Costs and LMI:** Australian buyers must account for state-based **Stamp Duty** (property transfer tax), which can require $20,000 to $45,000 in upfront cash. Additionally, borrowing more than 80% of property value (Loan-to-Value Ratio > 80%) requires **Lenders Mortgage Insurance (LMI)**.

## Common Homebuying Mistakes to Avoid

When stretching to buy a home, buyers frequently make critical financial errors that jeopardize their long-term stability:

* **1. Borrowing the Absolute Maximum Approved:** A lender's pre-approval represents the maximum debt you are legally permitted to take on, not what is comfortable for your household. Borrowing to the limit leaves you vulnerable if property taxes rise, work hours fluctuate, or family expenses increase.
* **2. Emptying Cash Reserves for the Down Payment:** Depleting every dollar in your checking and savings accounts to hit a purchase price target leaves you defenseless against sudden repairs or unexpected medical bills. Always retain at least 3 to 6 months of living expenses in an accessible emergency fund.
* **3. Overlooking Escrow and Tax Increases:** In many jurisdictions, property taxes are reassessed upon sale. If the previous owner had decades of tax caps or homestead exemptions, your first annual tax bill may jump hundreds of dollars per month above the listing estimate.
* **4. Forgetting Closing Costs:** Closing costs typically require **2% to 5% of the total loan amount** in cash at settlement (covering appraisal, title insurance, loan origination fees, transfer taxes, and initial escrow prepaids). Review our [first-time homebuyer checklist](/blog/homebuyer-first-mortgage) to prepare your documentation and funds in advance.
* **5. Taking on New Debt Before Closing:** Purchasing furniture on credit, opening a new credit card, or financing a new car between loan application and final settlement can alter your DTI ratio and cause your mortgage approval to be rescinded days before closing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much house can I afford on my salary using the 28/36 rule?
Under the traditional 28/36 guideline, your monthly housing payment (principal, interest, taxes, and insurance) should not exceed 28% of your gross monthly income, and your total monthly debt obligations (housing plus student loans, car payments, and credit cards) should not exceed 36%. For example, on a $100,000 annual salary ($8,333/month), your maximum target housing outlay under this rule is approximately $2,333 per month, supporting a home priced around $315,000 to $365,000 depending on down payment, interest rates, and local taxes.

### Can I qualify for a mortgage if my debt-to-income ratio is above 36%?
Yes. While 36% is a conservative personal finance guideline, many mortgage programs permit higher back-end debt-to-income (DTI) ratios. Conventional conforming loans backed by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac frequently allow DTIs up to 43% to 45% (and occasionally up to 50% with strong compensating factors such as high credit scores or significant cash reserves). FHA loans can approve DTIs up to 46.9% front-end and 56.9% back-end through automated underwriting systems. However, higher DTIs leave less discretionary room in your household budget.

### Should I base my home budget on gross salary or take-home pay?
Mortgage lenders legally evaluate your borrowing capacity using gross income (pre-tax salary). However, for your personal household cash flow, you should always verify affordability against your net take-home pay. Mandatory payroll taxes, health insurance premiums, and retirement contributions significantly reduce disposable cash, and an automated lender approval based on gross salary may leave you feeling "house-poor" on take-home pay.

### How does my existing monthly debt affect how much house I can buy?
Non-housing monthly debts—such as auto loans, minimum credit card payments, student loans, and personal loans—directly reduce your purchasing power through the back-end DTI cap. Because lenders limit your total monthly debt payments (typically to 36% to 45% of gross income), every $100 in existing monthly debt obligations reduces your allowable housing payment by $100, which can lower your home purchasing power by $13,000 to $16,000 at current interest rates.

### How do Canadian mortgage affordability rules differ from the US?
In Canada, federally regulated lenders evaluate affordability using Gross Debt Service (GDS) capped around 39% and Total Debt Service (TDS) capped around 44%. Additionally, the Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions (OSFI) mandates a mortgage stress test (Guideline B-20): borrowers must qualify at their contract rate plus 2.00%, or 5.25%—whichever is higher. Minimum down payments are 5% on the first $500,000, 10% on the portion up to $1.5 million, and 20% on higher prices, with default mortgage insurance required for down payments below 20%.

### How do Australian home loan serviceability rules work?
In Australia, the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA) requires banks to apply a minimum 3.00% interest rate buffer above the loan's contractual interest rate when assessing borrowing capacity. Lenders also review declared living expenses against the Household Expenditure Measure (HEM) benchmark and require Lenders Mortgage Insurance (LMI) when borrowing more than 80% of property value.

### Does a 20% down payment increase how much house I can buy on the same salary?
Yes, substantially. A 20% down payment increases purchasing power in three ways: it eliminates Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI), reduces the borrowed loan amount (lowering monthly interest and principal charges), and provides immediate equity that allows your monthly payment budget to support a higher overall purchase price.
