# How Much House Can I Afford? A Guide to Calculating Your Home Budget

Determining **how much house can I afford** is the most critical question when planning to buy a home. Whether you are a first-time homebuyer or preparing to upgrade, establishing a realistic **home buying budget** ensures you purchase a property that supports your lifestyle without creating ongoing financial strain.

To answer search intent directly: **how much house you can afford depends on four primary pillars**:
1. Your **gross monthly income** and net take-home pay.
2. Your existing **recurring debt commitments** (auto loans, student loans, credit cards).
3. Your available **cash for a down payment** and closing costs.
4. The **total monthly mortgage payment**, which encompasses principal, interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, and applicable private mortgage insurance (PMI) or HOA dues.

Under the traditional **28/36 rule**, personal finance experts recommend that your monthly housing outlay should not exceed **28% of your gross monthly income**, and your total debt obligations should stay within **36%**. For example, a household earning $100,000 annually ($8,333/month) with moderate debt can comfortably support a monthly housing payment of roughly $2,333, translating to an affordable home purchase price between $315,000 and $390,000 depending on interest rates and down payment.

Crucially, what a commercial lender pre-approves is your *maximum borrowing limit*, which is often higher than what you can *comfortably afford*. This guide breaks down the math, underwriting rules, hidden costs, and strategies to help you calculate your true home affordability.

---

## What Determines How Much House You Can Afford?

When searching for a home, looking only at list prices can be misleading. Mortgage underwriters and personal budgets operate on monthly cash flows. Six key factors determine **how much mortgage can I afford**:

* **Gross Income vs. Take-Home Pay:** Lenders evaluate gross income before taxes, but you pay bills with net take-home pay. Payroll taxes, medical insurance, and retirement deductions reduce disposable cash by 25% to 35%.
* **Debt-to-Income (DTI) Ratio:** Underwriters measure the share of your income already committed to recurring non-housing debt. Higher existing debt directly lowers your borrowing ceiling.
* **Down Payment Capital:** The liquid cash you invest upfront reduces the principal loan balance, lowers your monthly interest charges, and avoids PMI if you put down 20%.
* **Prevailing Interest Rates:** Because mortgages amortize over 15 to 30 years, small rate changes significantly alter borrowing capacity. A 1.0% rate increase shrinks purchasing power by roughly 10% on the same monthly payment.
* **Credit Score:** Your credit score dictates the interest rate and PMI pricing you receive. Higher scores qualify for prime rates, saving hundreds of dollars each month.
* **Property Taxes and Insurance:** Property taxes vary by county from under 0.6% to over 2.2% of assessed value. Homeowners insurance also varies by region, heavily impacting monthly escrow costs.

## How Much of Your Income Should Go Toward Housing?

A core dilemma in home buying is determining what percentage of your income should go toward housing. Spending too much leads to becoming **"house-rich and cash-poor"**—owning valuable real estate while struggling to fund basic living expenses, vehicle upkeep, or retirement savings.

Financial planners evaluate housing budget thresholds across standard guidelines:

| Target Allocation | Budget Framework | Financial Impact |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **20% to 25% of Take-Home Pay** | Conservative Budget | Maximizes discretionary savings, emergency funds, and retirement investing while minimizing stress. |
| **28% of Gross Income** | Traditional 28/36 Rule | Standard mortgage industry benchmark balancing homeownership with other financial commitments. |
| **30% of Gross Income** | Federal Standard (HUD) | The U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development considers spending over 30% to be housing cost-burdened. |
| **36% to 45% of Gross Income** | Extended Lender Cap | Permitted by flexible loan programs (FHA or conventional AUS), but leaves tight margins for unexpected life events. |

Because payroll deductions reduce your gross pay, allocating 28% of *gross income* to a mortgage often equals **35% to 40% of net take-home pay**. Always verify your prospective payment against your net paycheck before committing to a purchase.

## Understanding the 28/36 Rule

The **28/36 rule** is a classic underwriting guideline used to establish safe debt limits based on gross monthly income:

$$\text{Front-End DTI (Housing Ratio)} = \frac{\text{Monthly Housing Payment}}{\text{Gross Monthly Income}} \le 28\%$$

$$\text{Back-End DTI (Total Debt Ratio)} = \frac{\text{Total Monthly Debt Payments}}{\text{Gross Monthly Income}} \le 36\%$$

### 1. The Front-End Ratio (28% Cap)
The front-end ratio caps your proposed monthly housing payment—principal, interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, and HOA dues (PITI)—at **28% of gross monthly income**.

On a $96,000 annual salary ($8,000 monthly gross):

$$\text{Front-End Housing Ceiling} = \$8,000 \times 0.28 = \$2,240 \text{ per month}$$

### 2. The Back-End Ratio (36% Cap)
The back-end ratio includes your housing costs **plus all recurring non-housing debt** (car payments, student loans, credit card minimums, and personal loans), capping total monthly debt service at **36% of gross income**:

$$\text{Total Debt Ceiling} = \$8,000 \times 0.36 = \$2,880 \text{ per month}$$

If this household has $700 in monthly car and student loan payments, their allowable housing payment under the back-end rule is:

$$\$2,880 - \$700 = \$2,180 \text{ per month}$$

Because $2,180 is lower than the front-end limit ($2,240), the **back-end ratio binds**, capping their monthly housing budget at $2,180.

### Guideline vs. Lender Underwriting Standards
The 28/36 rule is a recommended budgeting framework, not an inflexible federal limit. Actual underwriting standards vary by loan program:
* **Conventional Loans (Fannie Mae & Freddie Mac):** Typically allow back-end DTIs up to **43% to 45%**, and up to **50%** with automated underwriting system (AUS) approval and compensating factors like strong credit or substantial cash reserves.
* **FHA Loans:** Standard caps are 31% front-end and 43% back-end, but automated approvals routinely permit back-end DTIs exceeding **50%**.
* **VA Loans:** Feature a 41% back-end benchmark with primary emphasis on residual income rather than a strict front-end cap.

While qualifying at a 45% DTI is legally permissible, following the 28/36 rule protects you against payment shock.

## How to Calculate Your Home Affordability Step by Step

Follow these seven steps to learn **how to calculate home affordability** accurately:

1. **Calculate Gross Monthly Income:** Total your annual pre-tax household earnings and divide by 12. A household earning $108,000 has a gross monthly income of **$9,000**.
2. **Sum Recurring Monthly Debts:** Add up all minimum monthly debt obligations: auto loans ($400), student loans ($250), and credit cards ($150), totaling **$800/month**.
3. **Apply 28/36 Ratios:**
   * Front-end cap: $\$9,000 \times 0.28 = \$2,520$
   * Back-end cap: $(\$9,000 \times 0.36) - \$800 = \$3,240 - \$800 = \$2,440$
   * The binding housing limit is **$2,440 per month**.
4. **Deduct Non-Loan Housing Costs:** Subtract estimated property taxes ($300/mo), homeowners insurance ($120/mo), and PMI ($120/mo), totaling $540. Remaining for principal and interest: $\$2,440 - \$540 = \mathbf{\$1,900/month}$.
5. **Convert P&I to Maximum Loan Amount:** At an illustrative 6.75% 30-year fixed rate (factor: ~$6.49 per $1,000 borrowed), an allowable P&I of $1,900 supports approximately **$292,800** in mortgage principal.
6. **Add Down Payment:** If you saved $33,000 (roughly 10% down), your target home price is: $\$292,800 + \$33,000 \approx \mathbf{\$325,800}$.
7. **Test Against Take-Home Pay:** Verify that the $2,440 monthly payment is within 30% of your actual net monthly take-home pay, ensuring adequate room for lifestyle and emergency savings.

## Important Costs Beyond Your Monthly Mortgage

A frequent misstep among buyers is calculating affordability based solely on loan principal and interest. The complete **monthly mortgage payment** includes several mandatory ongoing and upfront expenses:

### 1. Down Payment
Your down payment is the initial equity provided at settlement. Conventional loans allow as little as 3% to 5% down, and FHA loans require 3.5%. While 20% down eliminates mortgage insurance, saving that amount can delay buying. Finding the right balance between down payment size and preserving liquid reserves is essential.

### 2. Property Taxes
Assessed by local county and city governments to fund public education, roads, and emergency services, property taxes are escrowed monthly by your lender. Rates range from 0.5% of market value in low-tax jurisdictions to over 2.2% in higher-tax states. On a $350,000 home, that equals $145 to $640 per month.

### 3. Homeowners Insurance
Hazard insurance covers casualty damage from fire, wind, and liability. Premiums average $1,200 to $2,500 annually ($100 to $210 monthly) depending on region and home condition, and are collected via your lender escrow account.

### 4. Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI)
When putting down less than 20% on a conventional mortgage, lenders require PMI to protect against default. PMI costs roughly **0.3% to 1.5% of the loan amount annually** ($75 to $200/month on a $300,000 loan). Under the federal Homeowners Protection Act, you can request PMI removal once your loan balance reaches 80% of original property value. For a full breakdown, see our guide on [what is included in a monthly mortgage payment](/blog/understanding-mortgage-payments).

### 5. HOA Fees
Condominiums and planned communities charge mandatory Homeowners Association (HOA) dues for shared amenities, maintenance, and insurance reserves. Lenders include HOA dues directly in your front-end debt ratio; a $300 monthly HOA fee reduces your mortgage borrowing capacity by roughly $45,000.

### 6. Closing Costs
Settlement requires liquid cash for lender origination fees, appraisal, title search, insurance, recording taxes, and prepaid escrow reserves. Closing costs generally range between **2% and 5% of the loan amount** ($7,000 to $17,500 on a $350,000 loan), which must be paid separately from your down payment.

### 7. Maintenance and Repairs
Homeowners must handle all routine repairs and capital replacements. Budget **1% to 2% of the home value annually** ($3,500 to $7,000/year on a $350,000 property) in a dedicated maintenance reserve. Maintaining liquid emergency savings is crucial; learn more in our guide to [emergency fund size benchmarks](/blog/emergency-fund-size).

Borrowers with top-tier credit (760+) save **$417 per month** and **over $150,000 in total interest** compared to borrowers at the minimum conventional threshold (620). That $417 monthly savings can expand your purchasing budget by nearly $60,000. Furthermore, excellent credit significantly lowers monthly PMI premiums.

## Practical Tips to Increase Your Home Buying Budget

If your target price exceeds your current affordability calculation, these practical steps can expand your purchasing capacity:

1. **Pay Off Recurring Debt:** Eliminating non-housing monthly liabilities improves your back-end DTI dollar-for-dollar. Retiring a $400 monthly car payment expands your mortgage borrowing capacity by roughly $50,000.
2. **Improve Your Credit Score:** Paying down credit cards below 25% utilization and maintaining pristine payment history can lift your score by 30 to 50 points, lowering interest rates and PMI premiums.
3. **Save a Larger Down Payment:** Accumulating extra cash lowers the borrowed loan balance, reduces monthly interest charges, and eliminates PMI once you reach 20% equity.
4. **Compare Multiple Mortgage Lenders:** Research from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) and Freddie Mac shows that comparing quotes from three or more lenders saves borrowers an average of $3,000 to $5,000 in costs and interest over five years.
5. **Research First-Time Homebuyer Programs:** Many state housing finance agencies (HFAs) and HUD-sponsored programs provide down payment assistance grants and subsidized financing for eligible buyers.
6. **Target Lower Property Tax Areas:** Choosing a neighborhood with a 1.0% tax rate over a 2.0% area can save $300 to $400 monthly on a $400,000 home, instantly increasing your purchasing power.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much house can I afford based on my income?
Under the standard 28/36 rule, your monthly housing payment should not exceed 28% of your gross monthly income. On a $90,000 salary ($7,500/month), your target housing outlay is roughly $2,100 per month, supporting a home priced between $260,000 and $340,000 depending on down payment, interest rate, and property taxes.

### What is the 28/36 rule for home affordability?
The 28/36 rule suggests spending no more than 28% of your gross monthly income on housing costs (PITI + HOA) and no more than 36% on all recurring debt payments combined (housing plus auto loans, student loans, and credit cards).

### How does debt-to-income (DTI) ratio affect how much mortgage I can afford?
Your debt-to-income ratio measures your monthly debt commitments against pre-tax income. Because lenders enforce back-end DTI limits, every dollar committed to existing liabilities directly reduces the allowable mortgage payment you can qualify for.

### Can I qualify for a mortgage if my DTI is above 36%?
Yes. The 28/36 rule is a personal budgeting guideline. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac permit conventional DTIs up to 45% (and up to 50% with automated underwriting approval), while FHA loans allow DTIs above 50% with strong compensating factors.

### How much down payment do I really need to buy a house in the US?
You do not need 20% down. Conventional loans allow as little as 3% down for first-time buyers, FHA loans require 3.5%, and VA or USDA loans offer 0% down options. Putting 20% down eliminates Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI) and lowers monthly interest charges.

### Does my credit score affect how much house I can afford?
Yes. A higher credit score secures lower mortgage interest rates and cheaper PMI. On a $350,000 loan, an interest rate spread of 1.5% between excellent credit (760+) and fair credit (640) can increase your monthly payment by over $300, reducing your purchasing power.

### How much of my take-home pay should go toward housing?
Financial planners recommend keeping total monthly housing costs within 25% to 30% of net take-home pay. This accounts for income taxes, healthcare premiums, and retirement contributions, preventing you from becoming house-poor.

## Conclusion

Answering **how much house can I afford** is about finding a sustainable budget that supports your long-term financial health, rather than borrowing the maximum amount a lender permits.

Before beginning your home search:
* Calculate your front-end and back-end debt-to-income ratios using the 28/36 rule as your baseline.
* Account for full housing costs: principal, interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, PMI, HOA fees, and maintenance.
* Stress-test your monthly housing payment against your net take-home pay.
* Ensure you preserve three to six months of liquid living expenses after paying your down payment and closing costs.
